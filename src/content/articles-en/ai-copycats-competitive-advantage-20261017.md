On October 14, developer Pandorio Games shared footage of *Escape the Walls*, a game about punching through walls and clearing debris to escape. The next day, similar games had appeared on Roblox. According to an October 16 report by Game*Spark, users claiming to have produced some of the lookalikes said they had used Anthropic's Claude. The original clip had drawn about 4.5 million views by the time of that report.

The anger is understandable. Someone spends time turning a concept into a playable experience, shares a glimpse of it, and sees strikingly similar work almost immediately. Some observers reached a chilling conclusion: developers should stop showing ideas before launch.

But that conclusion is what troubles me most.

**Are we spending all our energy condemning imitation while refusing to reckon with the speed at which imitation has changed?**

I am not arguing that copyright infringement should be tolerated. Quite the opposite. We need to defend legitimate rights *and* design businesses on the assumption that visible features will be copied. The challenge applies to games, apps, websites, and software-as-a-service alike.

## The barrier between an idea and a product is shrinking

Copycats predate generative AI. Successful game mechanics inspire followers; competitors borrow app features; retailers study effective digital storefronts. Imitation has always been part of competition.

What used to provide some protection was the distance between imagining and executing. Delivering a working product takes engineering, design, infrastructure, testing, distribution, and money. Those demands give an early mover time.

AI is shortening parts of that journey. A screenshot, a workflow description, or an observed interaction can increasingly become the starting point for a quick prototype. That does not mean an AI-built copy is instantly secure, maintainable, reliable, or commercially viable. Production quality still requires substantial work. But even the ability to show a functioning lookalike quickly can change who gets attention first.

**The same technology that democratizes creation also democratizes imitation.** We cannot celebrate only one side of that equation.

## Not everything called “theft” is the same

A crucial distinction is often lost amid the anger. Game*Spark reported the appearance of lookalikes and statements by their purported makers. It did not establish through independent technical verification exactly which code was generated by AI, nor did it report a court finding that each game infringed copyright.

Similarity and infringement are not synonyms. The U.S. Copyright Office explains that a game's basic idea and methods of play are not protected by copyright, although sufficiently original textual or visual expression may be. Section 102(b) of U.S. copyright law similarly excludes ideas, methods, and systems themselves from copyright protection.

Japan's Agency for Cultural Affairs distinguishes the development and training of AI from the generation and use of outputs in its copyright guidance. Individual disputes can require analysis of protected expression, similarity, reliance on an existing work, and other circumstances. Trademarks, unfair competition rules, contracts, and trade-secret protection may also matter.

Borrowing a gameplay concept, reproducing protected artwork, impersonating a brand, and threatening a creator for payment are different acts. Compressing them all into a single word—“copying”—makes it harder to defend creators without criminalizing legitimate competition.

**Preparing for imitation does not mean excusing unlawful imitation.**

## Creation gets cheaper. Defense may not.

The next point is a business inference, not a measured industry-wide trend.

If cheap tools make it easier to produce many similar products, original developers may face more work identifying lookalikes, preserving evidence, assessing IP rights, submitting takedown requests, explaining authenticity to customers, and deciding whether legal action is worth the cost.

There is no reason to assume the cost of enforcing a right will fall as quickly as the cost of producing a prototype. A lookalike might appear in hours, while gathering evidence, locating the responsible party, and obtaining advice could take days or longer. By the time a dispute is resolved, the market's attention may have moved on.

That potential asymmetry matters most to small studios and independent developers. It is not proof that all businesses' legal costs are already rising. It is a risk that deserves a place in product strategy and financial planning.

## If secrecy becomes the only answer, small creators lose again

The reaction that stayed with me was not simply the accusation of plagiarism. It was the suggestion that developers should never share work in progress.

For small studios, early sharing is not vanity. It helps them find an audience without a large advertising budget, learn from reactions, and build a community before launch. Closing that channel may benefit established companies with existing reach at the expense of the very people AI is supposed to empower.

A tool that makes creation accessible should not make creators afraid to show what they are making. That is a deeper contradiction than the argument between two developers online.

The practical answer is thoughtful disclosure, not total silence. Show the experience, the vision, and the problems being solved. Protect unpublished implementation details, sensitive customer data, security information, and know-how that genuinely needs to remain confidential. Publication itself should become a deliberate part of the product strategy.

## For SaaS, the defensible value often sits beyond the screen

My work includes helping clinics improve operations and developing services built around messaging, forms, bookings, and digital workflows. That is why this story feels relevant far beyond gaming.

A rival may replicate a booking form or recreate the visible flow of a patient questionnaire. But a screenshot does not reveal why patients abandon a form, which staff tasks create bottlenecks, or what operational changes actually help a clinic. Real-world knowledge comes from repeated observation, implementation, support, and accountability.

If a product's only advantage is that it has a particular feature, the business may be forced to fight every new imitation. A more durable proposition combines features with demonstrated results, domain understanding, dependable support, and continuous improvement.

None of those advantages is permanent. AI can help competitors learn operational patterns too. Nor should companies create artificial switching costs or hoard personal information and call it a moat. Trust must be earned, not engineered through customer captivity.

Still, consistently solving a real problem is a different kind of competition from copying a screenshot.

## Decide what needs protection—and what can be copied

Building for a copyable world does not mean giving up. It means designing defense and growth together.

Establish ownership of original expression, branding, contractual rights, and trade secrets. Keep dated records of creation and publication. Identify which visible features competitors could easily reproduce and avoid relying on those features alone. Build feedback loops that turn customer experience into better products. And establish a proportional response to suspected infringement, based on harm, evidence, cost, and the likelihood of obtaining a useful remedy.

Protect what the law and good business practice can protect. At the same time, invest in value that copying a feature cannot immediately deliver.

## Criticism cannot reverse technology—but we still need it

I do not believe public criticism of malicious infringement is pointless. Without consequences for blatant copying of protected expression, creators—especially those with limited resources—may be forced to absorb unfair losses.

What feels unproductive is acting as though enough outrage could restore yesterday's development costs and entry barriers. It cannot. Businesses that spend all their energy longing for an uncopiable world risk losing sight of customers and what comes next.

Yet “just build the next thing” is not a fair answer either. It puts the burden of relentless reinvention on the people least able to afford it. Accessible enforcement procedures, transparent platform responses, and safeguards against deceptive impersonation matter as much as better business models.

**Can we reward the person who creates something original while preserving the freedom of others to improve on an idea?**

That tension goes beyond copyright. It is becoming a question of management, market design, and the future of creative work.

In a world where visible ideas can be replicated cheaply, what are we truly trying to protect? The code? The interface? The moment of inspiration?

Or the ongoing effort to make someone else's work or life meaningfully better?
