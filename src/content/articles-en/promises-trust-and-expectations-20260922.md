“Absolutely. I’d love to hear your proposal.”

You prepare, but the other person never replies to schedule a meeting.

A month after agreeing to a contract, someone says the price is too high.

A company hires someone without defining the job, then suddenly decides that the employee has failed to meet expectations.

A volunteer says, “I’ll be there to help,” only to cancel on the day.

These situations are not identical. People get sick; emergencies happen; legitimate reasons to revisit an agreement can arise. But what happens when the person who made the promise offers no explanation, while someone else bears the cost?

That is the friction I want to examine today.

## A promise enters someone else's calendar

When someone asks to hear a proposal, the other person researches, prepares, and reserves time. When someone promises to help, an organizer may plan around that commitment.

Perhaps the speaker meant only “I’m interested” or “I’ll come if I can.” If that is not made clear, one side starts acting on an assumption the other side never shared. The cost is not just one missed meeting. It includes preparation, opportunities passed up, and the effort of finding a replacement.

I do not assume that every unanswered message reflects bad intent. The problem is how easily the burden placed on the other person disappears from view.

## Interest is not an agreement

In business, an expression of interest is different from a confirmed meeting. A request to renegotiate a contract calls for an explanation and a new discussion, not an assumption that the other party will absorb the change. Hiring also requires a shared understanding of responsibilities and expected outcomes.

When people rely on “surely they understand,” both sides can later feel that the agreement was something entirely different.

## Trust people, but do not hand over your plans

This is also a reminder to myself.

I want to take people at their word. Relationships in work and community life would become bleak if every conversation began with suspicion.

But I can distinguish enthusiasm from a confirmed commitment. I can scale my preparation once a meeting date is fixed, confirm scope and terms in writing, build a little slack into volunteer plans, and set a response deadline before moving to another option.

That is not a strategy for distrusting people. It protects my time—and the time of people who do follow through.

I should hold myself to the same standard: do not make promises lightly; communicate early when circumstances change; and give a clear answer so the other person can decide what to do next.

## Trust grows through small acts

“Absolutely, let's do it” sounds encouraging. Trust, though, grows through the reply that arrives, the time that is kept, the terms that are clarified, and the explanation offered when plans change.

I will keep expecting good things from people. But I will not build my life on the assumption that everyone will act exactly as I hope.
